# A free copywork generator for your homeschool

Sunday night. The morning basket is planned, the read-aloud is picked, and the copywork notebook is empty — again — because the printable hunt ate the hour you had. A copywork generator ends that hunt: paste the passage you already chose, print a page sized to your kid, close the laptop.

That is the whole pitch. The rest of this page is what goes on that page, by age, and a week of passages so Monday starts without a search bar.

## What copywork actually is

Copywork is a page with a short, well-written passage on it — one sentence for a six-year-old, a paragraph for a twelve-year-old — and the child copies it in their best hand onto their own page. It came into homeschooling through Charlotte Mason-style education, where the idea is that the passage itself is worth the child's time: a line of poetry, a proverb, one clean sentence of history.

What it quietly practices: looking carefully, holding spacing across a line, capitals where they belong, punctuation that print worksheets usually skip. It is handwriting practice where the content is something you would keep anyway. It is not a cure for anything and it does not need to be long — ten quiet minutes beats a page of arguing.

## Copywork and handwriting practice are not the same job

Handwriting drills target letterforms: this is how an *f* goes, twenty times. Copywork targets the whole sentence: spacing, capitals, punctuation, starting at the margin. Both belong in a week, and the balance moves with age.

If the letters themselves are still shaky, a copywork page asks for too many skills at once. Start with [sight word tracing sheets](/blog/sight-word-tracing-worksheets) or plain letter practice, and let copywork arrive when single words come out clean. For most kids that is somewhere in the 6-to-7 range, with tracing first.

## What goes on a copywork page, by age

A copywork page has two parts: the model, printed once in a hand worth copying, and empty lines below it sized to the writer. What changes by age is not the idea — it is the length and the line size:

| Age | The passage | The page | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5–6** | One sentence, 3–5 words | One dark model line, then pale rows to trace over, on the tallest lines | 5 minutes |
| **7–8** | One full sentence with a capital and a period | Model strip on top, two or three empty lines under it | 5–8 minutes |
| **9–11** | Two sentences, or a two-line chunk of a poem | Model on top, empty lines at normal width | 10 minutes |
| **12+** | A short paragraph | Model sheet, copying onto wide-ruled or college-ruled paper | 10–15 minutes |

Tracing first is not a downgrade for the 5–6 row — it is how the passage gets read slowly, word by word, before the hand is asked to remember it.

## Build the page in five minutes, free

### The trace-then-copy page (ages 5–6)

1. Open the [name tracing generator](/name-tracing). The box takes any text, one line per entry — it is not limited to names.
2. Paste your passage on one line. Keep it to 3–5 words at this age.
3. Font: **Patrick Hand** for print. If your family starts cursive early, **Cedarville Cursive** works the same way.
4. Row height: **Kindergarten**. Dark example row on. Letter paper, download the PDF.

You get one dark model and pale rows beneath it — a complete copywork page from one paste.

### The model-and-lined-paper page (ages 7+)

1. Open the [cursive writing generator](/cursive) (or the main generator on the home page for print). Type the passage.
2. Export the PDF. That is your model sheet.
3. Print blank pages from [printable handwriting paper](/printable-paper) — three-line paper for 7–8, wide ruled later.
4. Staple the model on top of the lined pages. Your child copies underneath, one line per written line, and the notebook keeps itself all week.

Two printouts instead of one — that is the honest trade. The single-page version exists only for the trace format, because a dark model plus blank rows on one sheet is exactly what the tracing generator builds.

Swap in whatever your family is reading. The rule of thumb: the passage should be shorter than the child's patience, and worth saying out loud once.

## If copywork becomes a fight

Halve the passage before you negotiate anything else. Then:

- Offer a choice of two passages. Ownership is cheap to give and expensive to skip.
- Alternate days — copywork Monday, word work Tuesday — so no single page is the enemy.
- One tracing day a week: same passage, pale rows, different pencil color. It feels like a new job and practices the same sentence.

Ten minutes includes the complaining. If it regularly runs longer, the passage is too long.

## What the free pages will not do

The fonts are open-source — Patrick Hand and Cedarville Cursive — not the hand from your curriculum's workbook, so a few letterforms will differ. There are no numbered stroke arrows on the trace rows. The model page does not come with ruled lines attached for the copy work; you pair it with printed lined paper, or use the trace format when a single page matters more than the format.

## People also ask

- **What is copywork in homeschooling?** A short, well-written passage the child copies in their best hand onto their own page — one sentence at 5–6, growing to a paragraph by 12. It practices spacing, capitals, and punctuation on top of letterforms.
- **What age should copywork start?** Around 6, with tracing first: one dark model line plus pale rows to trace. Independent copying under a model fits most 7–8 year olds.
- **How long should a copywork passage be?** One 3–5 word sentence at 5–6, one full sentence at 7–8, two sentences or a poem chunk at 9–11, a short paragraph at 12+. Cap the session at 10–15 minutes.
- **How do I make free copywork pages?** Paste the passage into a generator that accepts any text, pick the font, size the rows to your child's age, and download the PDF — model page for older kids, trace page for beginners. No account or watermark on the tools above.
- **Is copywork the same as handwriting practice?** No. Handwriting drills isolate letterforms; copywork practices a whole sentence — spacing, capitals, punctuation. Most weeks have room for both.
