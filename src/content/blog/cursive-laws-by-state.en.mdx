# Cursive laws by state (2026): where US schools must teach it

If you teach elementary school in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, or Florida, a new line appeared in your standards documents this school year: cursive, by grade level, with your name on it. Parents are getting the same message sideways — a homework sheet in cursive, a note in the newsletter, a kid asking "why does my letter look funny?"

This page is the reference you'll want bookmarked: every state that requires cursive instruction as of October 2026, with the bill number, the effective date, and the grades involved — followed by a plain-English plan for the classroom or kitchen table if your state just joined the list.

## The quick answer

- **About 27 states** now require or explicitly encourage cursive — **18 with an actual law on the books**, the rest through state standards or state board requirements ([NEA, March 2026](https://www.nea.org/nea-today/all-news-articles/why-cursive-back-classroom); [FOX's independent count, September 2026](https://www.livenowfox.com/news/states-where-cursive-handwriting-is-still-taught-schools));
- **At least nine states have no requirement**: Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, and Wyoming. (Indiana and Missouri are the two most often mislisted as "required" — both have bills pending, neither has passed.)
- **The wave is recent and still moving.** Oklahoma and Kentucky in 2024, then New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Florida in 2026 — on top of California's law, effective January 2024.

### No statewide requirement

**Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, Wyoming** — no cursive requirement; districts decide. Indiana is the cautionary example for parents who trust a single source: it's listed as "required" on several tracking maps, but the legislature has voted down repeat attempts, and a 2023 bill was watered down to a reporting requirement. In most of the remaining states it's simply a local decision — New York, for instance, encourages cursive without mandating it, while New York City's district sets its own rule.

## What "required" actually means (the three flavors)

Laws look alike in headlines but do different things in classrooms:

1. **Statute (a law passed by the legislature)** — the strongest form. A bill names cursive, names grades, and districts must comply. California's AB 446 and Florida's 2026 law are in this category;
2. **State standards (adopted by the state board/education agency)** — cursive appears in grade-level ELA standards. Texas is the classic example: cursive from grade 2, with students expected to complete compositions in cursive by grade 5. No new law needed — it's already the rule;
3. **Encouraged / recommended** — guidance or model curriculum mentions cursive without a grade-level mandate. This is the category that makes the counts disagree.

The practical difference for a teacher: standards and statutes tend to come with **grade-level benchmarks** ("by the end of grade 3"), while "encouraged" usually means the decision drifts to the district or the individual teacher — which is how cursive quietly disappears.

## The 2024–2026 wave, in one timeline

Cursive left most US classrooms after the 2010 Common Core standards set keyboarding expectations and skipped handwriting entirely. The comeback started around 2016; the current wave is the fastest stretch yet:

- **Jul 2023 — New Hampshire** (HB 170): cursive and multiplication facts, both by the end of grade 5;
- **Jan 2024 — California** (AB 446): cursive instruction required in grades 1–6, the biggest state to mandate it in decades;
- **2024 — Kentucky** (SB 167): cursive proficiency by the end of grade 5, phased in from the 2025–26 school year. **Oklahoma** (HB 3727) required it in grades 3–5 starting the same school year;
- **2026 — New Jersey** (signed January, effective 2026–27): cursive in grades 3–5. **Pennsylvania** (Act 2, effective April): a requirement covering public *and private* elementary schools — one of the few to reach private classrooms. **Florida** (SB 182, effective July 1): grades 3–5 with a proficiency checkpoint before middle school.

Two more years like this and the "states without cursive" list will be shorter than the other one.

## Your state just added cursive: the Monday-morning plan

Nobody hands you a curriculum with the bill. Here's the four-week cycle elementary teachers land on when cursive shows up mid-year — it works for grade 2 (usually the starting grade) and for grade 4–6 catch-up groups, with one change:

1. **Week 1 — lowercase ovals and waves**: *c, a, d, g, o, q*, then *i, t, u, e, w*. One stroke family per day, one line of practice per kid. Type the day's letters into the [cursive worksheets generator](/cursive-worksheets), pick **Cedarville Cursive**, set row height to the **Grade 2 preset** (or 90px on the slider), print one sheet per student;
2. **Week 2 — bumps and first words**: *n, m, h, b, r, p*, then real words: *man, ham, him, run*. Kids connect letters for the first time this week — make it a ceremony, not a correction session;
3. **Week 3 — loops and their names**: *l, k, f, j*, then each child's own name. The [name tracing generator](/name-tracing) prints any name as an example row plus tracing rows — seven-year-olds and ten-year-olds both treat their own name as the event of the week;
4. **Week 4 — sentences and stamina**: two-sentence paragraphs using the week's letters. The [daily cursive practice page](/daily-cursive-handwriting-practice) prints a date-stamped warm-up sheet (strokes, letters, words, sentence) so the routine survives week 5.

**The grade-level difference that matters**: grades 2–3 are learning letter *formation* — big rows, fat pencils, celebration over neatness. Grades 4–6 are usually *relearning or catching up* — smaller rows, real content (spelling words, science vocabulary), and speed as the goal. Same generator, different settings: Grade 2 preset and celebration for the young ones; 68–80px rows and their current word list for the older groups.

Ten minutes, three times a week, is enough — that's the same schedule that works for [teaching cursive at home](/blog/how-to-teach-cursive-kids), and it fits in a morning-work slot.

## People also ask

**Which states do not require cursive in school?**
As of October 2026: Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Vermont, and Wyoming have no cursive requirement — districts there may still teach it by local choice, and Minnesota and Missouri both have bills pending.

**Does my state test cursive?**
Almost never directly. Florida's 2026 law is the outlier with a proficiency checkpoint before middle school; Kentucky sets an "end of grade 5" proficiency expectation. Everywhere else, cursive lives in standards that appear — if at all — inside broader English language arts assessment.

**Do private schools have to teach cursive too?**
Usually not — most mandates bind public schools. Pennsylvania's 2026 law is the notable exception, covering private elementary schools as well.

**Why did schools stop teaching cursive in the first place?**
The 2010 Common Core State Standards required keyboarding and left cursive out, and state education agencies followed the document they were given. The comeback began around 2016 — the full story is in [is cursive still taught in schools?](/blog/is-cursive-still-taught).
