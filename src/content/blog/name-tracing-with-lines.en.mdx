## What the three lines actually teach

A tracing worksheet with lines gives every letter a address system:

- **The top line** — where tall letters (M, h, l) stop *just short* of. Reaching for it is what makes capitals look confident instead of startled;
- **The dashed middle line** — the waistline. Most lowercase letters live between here and the bottom;
- **The bottom line** — the baseline. Everything stands on it; the "drifting baseline" problem in adult handwriting starts here too.

I walk through the full letter-family system (tall letters, small letters, tail letters) in [kindergarten handwriting paper, explained](/kindergarten-handwriting-paper). The short version for name tracing: **the lines are the teaching**. A child who fills a ruled band correctly is doing three jobs at once — sizing, spacing, and orienting — without anyone saying "slow down."

One honest note, because I get this question at every parent conference: the tracing letters on our sheets are **dotted outlines**, not light-gray solids, and each page leads with a **dark model row** in solid print. The model row is the target; the dotted rows are guided practice; the last rows are the transfer test.

## Which row height fits which age

This is the setting parents get wrong most often. Bigger is not easier forever — a band that's too tall makes letters float around inside it. Match the row height to the writer:

| Writer | Row height | Labeled on the tool | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preschool / Pre-K (3–4) | ~1.25 in | "Pre-K" | Whole-arm letter movements, plenty of room to overshoot |
| Kindergarten (5–6) | ~0.94 in | "Kindergarten" (default) | The classic worksheet band most K classrooms use |
| Grade 2+ (7–8) | ~0.71 in | "Grade 2" | Smaller, tighter spacing — for writers who already control size |

The upgrade rule I use in class: when a child fills the band top-to-bottom on most letters without prompting, drop one size. The struggle rule runs the other way — if every row is a fight, the band is too small. Go up, not back.

## When the child fights the pencil

Every classroom has one, and every family meets them eventually. When tracing turns into a standoff, I change the conditions before I change the expectations:

- **Air-trace first.** Write the name in the air with a giant invisible pencil, whole arm, twice. Then one row with a fat crayon, then pencil. Finger-tracing the dotted outlines counts as practice — the motor plan is the point;
- **Let them choose the name.** The dog's name, grandma's name, a superhero's name. Motivation first, repetitions second — the format does the teaching either way;
- **One line per day beats a full page on Sunday.** Two minutes, done while the paint dries. Stop while it's still fun; a traced name should never cost tears twice in one week;
- **Check the pencil, not the child.** A chubby starter pencil and a page that doesn't slide around fix more "attitude" problems than any pep talk.

And if today just doesn't work — put the sheet on the fridge, trace one name at dinner, and call it done. The lines will be there tomorrow.

## Two questions I hear every week

**"Dotted outlines or light-gray tracing letters — which is better?"**
Both teach. What matters is the sequence: a dark model to look at, guided rows to trace, then an empty band to try alone. Our sheets do model → dotted → empty bands on the same page, so the last row is honest work, not tracing theater.

**"Can I put more than one name on a sheet?"**
Yes — one per line, and each gets its own model row. For homework packets I type the full first name on line one and the nickname on line two. For the whole class, print one page per child; the generator keeps every setting.
