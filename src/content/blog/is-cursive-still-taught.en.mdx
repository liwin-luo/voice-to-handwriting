# Is cursive still taught in schools? What changed, and what's next

Short answer: **yes, in more states than five years ago — but it depends where you live.**

Long answer: cursive was quietly dropped from most US classrooms for about a decade, and has been loudly coming back since 2016. Here's the actual timeline, the states that require it now, and what the research does (and doesn't) say — because the cursive debate attracts a lot of overclaiming on both sides.

## How cursive left the classroom

In 2010, the **Common Core State Standards** — adopted by 40+ states — set grade-level expectations for English language arts. They required keyboarding proficiency. They did **not** mention cursive. State education agencies, implementing standards on tight schedules, largely followed suit: districts stopped buying cursive workbooks, teacher-prep programs stopped teaching how to teach it, and by the mid-2010s many elementary students never got a cursive unit at all.

Then the pendulum swung back. Starting around 2016, state after state wrote cursive back into their standards — a movement often attributed to a mix of teacher advocacy, historical-document literacy, and concerns about fine-motor development.

## Where cursive is required now

More than **twenty states** have restored cursive requirements (the count varies slightly by how you score "requirement" — check your own state's ELA standards for the current language). Notable examples:

- **California** — AB 446, signed in October 2023 and effective from January 2024, requires cursive instruction in **grades 1–6** ([California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov));
- **Texas** — state ELA standards have students learning cursive through elementary school and **writing legibly in cursive by fourth grade** ([Texas Education Agency](https://tea.texas.gov));
- **Arizona, Arkansas, Louisiana, North Carolina, Tennessee, Virginia, Kentucky and South Carolina**, among others, have cursive mandates or recommended standards in elementary grades.

The pattern to know as a parent: even where cursive is required, it often gets **a few weeks in one grade** rather than the multi-year drilling older generations had. That's why "my kid's school teaches cursive" and "my kid can actually write cursive" are frequently different claims.

## What research actually says (and doesn't)

- **Handwriting builds letter knowledge in young children.** A 2012 fMRI study by Karin James and Laura Engelhardt at Indiana University (*Trends in Neuroscience and Education*) found that children who practiced *forming* letters by hand showed stronger activation in reading-related brain networks than children who only traced or typed them;
- **Fluency helps composition.** Research by Virginia Berninger at the University of Washington found elementary students tended to write more words, faster, when composing essays by hand than by keyboard;
- **The "handwritten notes are magic" claim is overstated.** The famous 2014 "pen is mightier than the keyboard" study (Mueller & Oppenheimer, *Psychological Science*) found longhand note-takers outperformed laptop note-takers on conceptual questions — but later replication attempts have been mixed, so treat it as suggestive, not settled;
- **No strong evidence cursive beats print.** For legibility and speed, studies generally find practiced *printers* and practiced *cursive writers* land in similar territory. The benefits above come from **forming letters by hand at all**, not from cursive specifically.

Honest takeaway: cursive is a worthwhile skill — for reading grandma's letters, signing documents, writing a faster personal hand, and getting the fine-motor practice that letter formation provides. It is not a superpower, and adults who "must relearn cursive for their brain" are being sold something.

## What to do about it, by situation

- **Parent in a cursive-requirement state**: ask when it's taught and whether it's more than a one-week unit. If it's thin, 10 minutes a week at home covers the gap — see [how to teach cursive at home](/blog/how-to-teach-cursive-kids);
- **Parent in a state without requirements**: cursive is optional, so make it optional fun — a "grown-up writing style" novelty rather than homework;
- **Adult who never learned it**: the stroke-first method takes about a month of short sessions — [how to write in cursive](/blog/how-to-write-in-cursive) from zero;
- **Anyone practicing**: type words into the [cursive writing generator](/cursive), print, and trace — the same model-tracing loop classrooms use.

## People also ask

- **Do schools still teach cursive in 2026?** In 20+ US states, yes — it's written into elementary ELA standards. Elsewhere it varies by district, and even where required, instruction is often brief.
- **Why did schools stop teaching cursive?** The 2010 Common Core standards included keyboarding but omitted cursive, so most states dropped it during adoption; standards revisions since 2016 have been adding it back.
- **Is cursive required in California and Texas?** California requires cursive instruction in grades 1–6 (since 2024); Texas standards have students writing legibly in cursive by fourth grade.
- **Does learning cursive improve brain development?** Forming letters by hand is linked to stronger reading-network activation in young children — but that applies to handwriting generally, and cursive specifically hasn't been shown to beat print.
