# The cursive alphabet, organized the way you'll learn it

Alphabet charts sort cursive letters A to Z. That's great for reference and useless for learning — because cursive letters aren't 26 shapes, they're **about six strokes wearing different outfits**.

Here's every lowercase and capital, grouped into the stroke families handwriting programs teach, with the traps called out. When you're ready to practice, type any row of letters into the [cursive writing generator](/cursive) and print it as a free chart to trace.

## Lowercase letters by stroke family

| Family | Letters | What they share | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ovals** | a c d g o q | A round "c" base with different exits | *g* and *q* dive below the line — same dive, different tails |
| **Waves** | i t u w e | Sawtooth up-down strokes | *t* is taller than *i* — the difference is half the letter's height |
| **Bumps** | n m h b r p | Arches on the baseline | *b* starts like *l*, *p* starts with a separate downstroke in most styles |
| **Loops** | l f k j | Tall or diving loops | *j* and *f* are the only common letters that dive below; *f* often does both |
| **Finishers** | s v x y z | Individual shapes | *s* is the most-mangled letter in cursive — slow down for it |

Notice what's missing: alphabet order. Practicing *c → a → d → g → o → q* in one session teaches your hand one circle with six exits; practicing *a → b → c* teaches it three unrelated motions.

## Capitals, honestly

Cursive capitals are the least standardized part of cursive — every penmanship era drew them differently, and personal style shows first in capitals. Practical rules:

- Most capitals **don't connect** to the next letter. Formal styles connect some (like *A, C, E* with long tails), everyday styles rarely bother;
- Learn the **8 workhorse capitals** you'll actually write — *C, L, M, N, S, T, J, R* — before the ornamental ones;
- Capital *G* and *Z* in many traditional styles look nothing like their lowercase versions. That's not you doing it wrong; that's history.

## The letters that cause 90% of mistakes

1. **b vs l** — *b* is an *l* with a bump after the loop. Write the *l* completely, then retrace up to add the bump;
2. **o, b, v, w connections** — these four end high, so the next letter usually starts fresh. Forcing them to connect creates the mushy joins beginners hate;
3. **r** — the cursive *r* is a small shoulder, not a spike. Most "ugly cursive r" complaints are just a rushed shoulder;
4. **t vs i height** — the crossing bar of *t* sits at the *x*-height line; if your *t* matches your *i*, your words read as *i i n* instead of *tin*;
5. **s** — slow down. It's a baseline-level wave with a twist at the end, and it's the letter that most benefits from tracing a printed model a dozen times.

## People also ask

- **How many letters are in the cursive alphabet?** The same 26 — cursive is a style of writing the Latin alphabet, not a different one.
- **Which cursive letters are hardest?** Consistently: *s* (twist), *r* (shoulder), *b/l* confusion, and the four high-ending letters *o, b, v, w* that don't connect smoothly.
- **Do cursive capital letters connect to lowercase?** Mostly no in everyday styles — a few formal styles connect certain capitals, but everyday cursive lets capitals stand alone.
